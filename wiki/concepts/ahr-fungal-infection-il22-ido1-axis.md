## Definition

AHR's role in fungal infection is strongly tissue- and species-dependent. In Paracoccidioides brasiliensis (lung paracoccidioidomycosis) and vulvovaginal candidiasis, AHR is protective (Ahr-KO mice show worse fungal burden, lung lesions or reduced IL-22). In oropharyngeal candidiasis, AHR is detrimental — required for C. albicans-driven SRC-EGFR-endocytosis-mediated epithelial invasion (CH-223191 reduces disease). In Malassezia infection, fungus-derived AHR ligands (indirubin, indolo[3,2-b]carbazole, malassezin, tryptanthrin) suppress monocyte-derived dendritic cell maturation. In Aspergillus fumigatus, fungal IDO genes drive Trp catabolism that engages host AHR.

## Intuition

The same receptor, opposite outcomes in different mucosa: AHR is protective in vagina but harmful in oral cavity for the same pathogen (C. albicans). The mechanistic basis lies in tissue-specific availability of co-factors (SRC kinases, EGFR for invasion) and downstream cytokines (IL-22 for protection). Malassezia and Aspergillus exploit AHR-ligand chemistry differently — Malassezia produces direct AHR ligands; Aspergillus uses its own IDO to push host Trp metabolism into AHR-activating territory.

## When to use

- When mapping AHR effects across mucocutaneous fungal disease
- For designing tissue-specific AHR-targeted antifungal strategies
- When discussing the AHR-IDO1 axis as a generalisable antifungal node

## Known limitations

- Most evidence is murine; human translation is limited
- No parasite-derived AHR ligand identified; the IDO1-AHR axis in fungal pathogens may reflect a host-driven mechanism rather than direct microbial signalling

## Open problems

- Why does AHR drive opposing outcomes in oral vs vaginal candidiasis?
- Can AHR-IDO1 modulation augment antifungal therapy in immunocompromised patients?
- Are Malassezia AHR ligands relevant to seborrheic dermatitis pathogenesis?
