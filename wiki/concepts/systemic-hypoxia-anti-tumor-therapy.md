## Definition

The therapeutic use of whole-body (systemic) hypoxia — continuous low inhaled oxygen, e.g. 8% O2, or a pharmacological hemoglobin-oxygen affinity modulator — to suppress solid tumor growth. Distinct from, and opposite in effect to, the local intratumoral hypoxia that arises from poor perfusion and is a classical negative prognostic factor.

## Intuition

When only patches of a tumor are hypoxic, well-oxygenated regions perform energetically demanding biosynthesis (e.g. nucleotide synthesis) and supply salvageable substrates to hypoxic regions — a 'division of labor' that supports aggression. When the whole host/tumor is hypoxic, this division collapses, producing tumor-wide nucleotide depletion and slowed growth.

_Not applicable._

## Variants

Inhaled continuous hypoxia (8% O2); moderate hypoxia (11% O2, intermediate effect); pharmacological hypoxia via [[hypoxystat-hemoglobin-oxygen-affinity-modulator]]; combination with chemotherapy or immune checkpoint blockade.

## Comparison

Contrasts with [[hypoxia-activated-prodrugs-haps]] (which exploit local tumor hypoxia) and with the long-standing view that tumor hypoxia drives radioresistance and aggression (see [[hypoxia-radioresistance-oxygen-fixation]]).

## When to use

As a conceptual frame for host-environment / oxygen manipulation as an anti-cancer strategy and for interpreting altitude-cancer epidemiology.

## Known limitations

Demonstrated only in preclinical mouse models (single preprint); effect heterogeneous across cancer lineages (ccRCC may resist); systemic hypoxia carries risks (pulmonary hypertension, erythrocytosis, edema).

## Open problems

Mechanistic contribution of tumor-extrinsic factors (immune surveillance, exogenous nutrients); translatability and therapeutic window in humans.
