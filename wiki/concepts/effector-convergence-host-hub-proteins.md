## Definition

The observation that T3SS effectors from many different bacterial strains converge on a
small set of shared human target proteins far more than expected by chance, marking
those "convergence proteins" (notably members of the NF-κB module) as functionally
important hubs of microorganism–host interaction.

## Intuition

If unrelated bacteria independently evolved to target the same host protein, that
protein is probably a high-value control point of host biology.

## Formal notation

60 human proteins are subject to effector convergence; convergence on few host
interactors and targeting by effectors from ≥4 strains are both significant
(permutation tests, P < 0.0001 and P = 0.004).

## Variants

Convergence at the single-protein level vs at the network-module level (e.g. NF-κB,
SAPK/JNK).

## Comparison

Mirrors convergence signatures reported in plant–pathogen and plant–mutualist effector
interactomes; partial overlap with pathogen effector targets (12 shared human proteins).

## When to use

When prioritizing host proteins most likely to be biologically pivotal for
microbiome–host interaction.

## Known limitations

Convergence is inferred from one interactome map with finite sampling sensitivity.

## Open problems

Functional consequences at convergence hubs in commensal vs pathogenic contexts.
