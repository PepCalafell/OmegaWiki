## Definition

Dietary tryptophan is metabolised by the commensal microbiota (via bacterial tryptophanase and other enzymes) into AHR agonists including indole-3-aldehyde (I3A), indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), tryptamine (TA), indoxyl-3-sulfate (I3S), and other indole derivatives. These ligands sustain AHR signalling in intestinal epithelial cells, ILC3s, and Th17/Th22 cells, supporting IL-22 production, antimicrobial peptide expression, and barrier homeostasis. Loss of microbial AHR-ligand production (CARD9 mutations, antibiotic-driven dysbiosis, low-Trp diet) impairs barrier function and promotes inflammation.

## Intuition

The gut microbiome runs a continuous "AHR power supply" by converting tryptophan into AHR ligands. Lose the supply and barrier immunity collapses; restore it (probiotics, dietary ligands, post-biotics) and barrier homeostasis returns — the rationale for many AHR-targeting probiotic strategies.

## Variants

- Lactobacillus reuteri / johnsonii indole producers
- Bacterial tryptophanase → indole → host metabolism to I3S
- Cruciferous-vegetable I3C → acid stomach → DIM / ICZ → AHR
- Indigo naturalis (Qing-Dai) indigoid AHR agonists (clinical UC trials)
- Inulin / dietary fibre as AHR-ligand precursors

## When to use

- Interpreting microbiome-disease associations in IBD/MS through an AHR lens
- Designing probiotic / postbiotic AHR-agonist strategies (Lactobacillus reuteri, indole-producing engineered bacteria)
- Connecting diet/microbiome data to AHR-driven IL-22 / Treg / Tr1 outputs

## Known limitations

- AHR-ligand concentrations in vivo difficult to measure
- Inter-individual microbiome variability complicates therapy design

## Open problems

- Quantitative AHR-ligand fluxes at the mucosa
- Engineering microbial strains that produce defined, dose-controlled AHR agonists
