## Definition

The Human Genome Project (HGP) was the international effort, running from 1990 to 2003, to sequence and map the full set of human DNA. Its first draft was published in 2001 — simultaneously by the public International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium (Nature 409, 860–921) and the private Celera team led by J. Craig Venter (Science 291, 1304–1351).

## Intuition

The HGP is popularly seen as the *start* of the hunt for protein-coding genes, but it was closer to the *end* of a decades-long search (the first protein-coding gene, SCT/secretin, was inferred in 1902). Its enduring contribution was a comprehensive catalogue of ~20,000 protein-coding genes, which then served as the scaffold for studying the much larger non-coding and regulatory portion of the genome.

## Formal notation

Not applicable — a sequencing/cataloguing program rather than a formal model.

## Key variants

- Public consortium draft (Nature 2001) vs. Celera draft (Science 2001).
- Follow-on cataloguing efforts that built on the HGP scaffold: ENCODE, the International HapMap Project, and the 1000 Genomes Project.

## Known limitations

- A "complete" gene count remained contested: there is no consensus on where a gene starts and ends, and multiple naming conventions hinder cross-database linkage.
- The catalogue of components (parts list) is necessary but not sufficient to understand biological function, which arises from interactions between components.

## Open problems

- Disentangling which genes get studied because they are biologically important versus because of social "rich-gets-richer" research dynamics.
- Functional annotation of the still-uncharacterized majority of the genome.

## Relevance to active research

The HGP catalogue underpins essentially all modern genomics, GWAS, and network-medicine analyses. The framing that the HGP's value lies in the *network era* it enabled — rather than the protein catalogue itself — is the central argument of [[wealth-discovery-built-human-genome-project]].
