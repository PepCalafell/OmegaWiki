## Definition

BCG is a live attenuated strain of *Mycobacterium bovis* used as a vaccine against tuberculosis. Beyond its TB-specific protection, intradermal BCG is the prototypical inducer of trained immunity, conferring heterologous (non-specific) protection against unrelated pathogens.

## Intuition

A single intradermal dose reprograms myeloid cells and their progenitors, raising ex vivo cytokine responses to unrelated stimuli (e.g. *S. aureus*, *C. albicans*) for months. This off-target, beneficial non-specific effect motivates BCG's use as a model trained-immunity stimulus in humans.

## Formal notation

Standard adult research dose: 0.1 mL intradermal in the upper arm. Trained-immunity studies sample blood at baseline (T0), ~14 days (T14), and ~90 days (T90) post-vaccination.

## Key variants

- BCG strains: Bulgaria, Danish, Pasteur, Tokyo, Russia (differ in trained-immunity potency)
- Neonatal vs adult vaccination; single dose vs booster/revaccination

## Known limitations

Variable efficacy against pulmonary TB; strain- and dose-dependent trained-immunity effects; sex- and age-dependent responses complicate interpretation.

## Open problems

Mechanistic basis of durable epigenetic memory, optimal dosing for non-specific protection, and predictors of inter-individual response heterogeneity.

## Relevance to active research

The canonical human model for studying epigenetic (histone and DNA-methylation) encoding of innate immune memory and its genetic/sex modifiers.
