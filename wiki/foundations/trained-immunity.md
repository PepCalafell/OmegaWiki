## Definition

Trained immunity is the de facto memory of the innate immune system: a long-term functional reprogramming of innate immune cells (monocytes/macrophages, NK cells, neutrophils) and their bone-marrow progenitors, induced by an initial insult (e.g. BCG, β-glucan, infection), that results in an altered — usually enhanced — response to a subsequent, often unrelated (heterologous) challenge.

## Intuition

Classical immunology held that only adaptive lymphocytes form immunological memory. Trained immunity shows that innate cells "remember" prior exposure through epigenetic and metabolic rewiring rather than antigen-specific receptor rearrangement, giving non-specific cross-protection against unrelated pathogens.

## Formal notation

Operationally quantified as the fold change in ex vivo cytokine production (e.g. TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IFN-γ) upon heterologous restimulation after versus before the training stimulus. A FC > 1 indicates a trained (enhanced) response; FC < 1 indicates tolerance.

## Key variants

- Innate training (enhanced response) vs innate tolerance (suppressed response)
- Central trained immunity (bone-marrow progenitor reprogramming) vs peripheral
- Inducers: BCG, β-glucan, oxLDL, mevalonate, infection

## Known limitations

Whole-blood/PBMC ex vivo readouts mix cell types; the durability, heritability across cell generations, and translation to clinical protection remain partly unresolved.

## Open problems

Molecular basis of long-term maintenance (histone marks vs DNA methylation), inter-individual variability, and whether training can be therapeutically tuned for vaccination or sepsis.

## Relevance to active research

Central organizing concept for BCG immunology, vaccine adjuvancy, and host-directed therapy. The framework underpins studies dissecting whether DNA methylation, not only histone modification, encodes innate immune memory.
