## Definition
Latent enhancers are genomic regions that lack the canonical enhancer histone marks (H3K4me1) and transcription-factor binding in unstimulated cells, but acquire them de novo upon stimulation. Once established, many retain H3K4me1 after the signal subsides, providing a form of epigenetic memory that can fortify or accelerate subsequent stimulus-specific responses.

## Intuition
Unlike pre-marked "poised" enhancers, latent enhancers are not pre-configured; the stimulus itself instructs their creation, often via signal-dependent TFs that recruit pioneer/lineage factors. They explain how cells expand their enhancer repertoire in a microenvironment-specific manner.

## Formal notation
Operationally: regions gaining H3K4me1 (± H3K27ac) and TF binding only after stimulation, absent in naive cells.

## Key variants
- Memory-retaining latent enhancers (persist after signal removal)
- Transient latent enhancers (decommissioned after signal removal)

## Known limitations
- Distinguishing genuinely "latent" elements from low-signal pre-existing elements is threshold-dependent
- Memory persistence varies by stimulus and locus

## Open problems
- Mechanisms that decide retention versus decommissioning
- Whether latent enhancer memory contributes to trained immunity in vivo

## Relevance to active research
In human inflammatory macrophages, a fraction of activation-specific enhancers are bound independently of the lineage factor PU.1 and are proposed to be the human equivalent of murine latent enhancers.
