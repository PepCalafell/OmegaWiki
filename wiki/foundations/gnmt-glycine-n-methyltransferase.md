## Definition

GNMT catalyses the SAM-dependent methylation of glycine to sarcosine (N-methylglycine), releasing SAH. GNMT is highly expressed in liver, exocrine pancreas and kidney; it consumes SAM and is the dominant route for short-term regulation of cellular SAM/SAH ratios and sarcosine production.

## Intuition

GNMT acts as a SAM "overflow valve": it converts excess SAM into sarcosine to maintain SAM/SAH homeostasis. Sarcosine itself is normally low-abundance; its elevation is a marker of GNMT activity or downstream conversion impairment. In cancer cachexia, sarcosine accumulates across multiple tissues — implicating GNMT among other methyltransferases as part of the activated one-carbon arm.

## Formal notation

- Reaction: glycine + SAM → sarcosine (N-methylglycine) + SAH.
- Sarcosine downstream: oxidised back to glycine by sarcosine dehydrogenase (mitochondrial), feeding the one-carbon pool.
- Tissue distribution: liver, kidney, exocrine pancreas (high baseline).
- Clinical relevance: GNMT KO mice develop HCC; GNMT polymorphisms linked to liver disease.

## Key variants

- Hepatic GNMT (dominant tissue expression).
- Sarcosine as a methylation-flux biomarker (also implicated controversially in prostate cancer aggressiveness).

## Known limitations

- Specific GNMT inhibitors not widely available.
- Sarcosine has multiple metabolic fates beyond GNMT product — interpretation requires context.

## Open problems

- Whether GNMT-specific KO in tumour-bearing mice modulates cachexia phenotypes.
- Relative contribution of GNMT vs other methyltransferases to circulating sarcosine elevation in cachexia.
