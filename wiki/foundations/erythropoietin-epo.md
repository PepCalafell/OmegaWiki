## Definition

Erythropoietin (EPO) is a glycoprotein hormone that drives red blood cell production (erythropoiesis). Its gene is a classic HIF target: hypoxia-stabilized HIF (predominantly HIF2) induces EPO transcription in interstitial renal fibroblasts and hepatocytes, coupling tissue oxygen sensing to systemic oxygen-carrying capacity.

## Intuition

EPO is the historical paradigm of an oxygen-regulated gene — the search for the factor controlling its hypoxic induction is what led to the discovery of HIF. It is the canonical physiological readout of HIF pathway activation in vivo and the clinical endpoint that validated HIF prolyl-hydroxylase inhibitors for renal anaemia.

## Formal notation

Hypoxia → HIF2α stabilization → EPO gene transcription (kidney/liver) → erythropoiesis ↑ → blood oxygen-carrying capacity ↑.

## Key variants

- Recombinant human EPO (epoetin alfa/beta) — established anaemia therapeutic, with thrombotic and other side effects in some patients.
- Endogenous EPO induction via HIF prolyl-hydroxylase inhibitors (roxadustat, daprodustat, vadadustat) — an alternative to recombinant EPO for CKD-associated anaemia.

## Known limitations

- Elevated serum EPO from systemic hydroxylase inhibition can cause unwanted erythropoiesis in non-anaemic patients — a key reason for pursuing tissue-targeted HIF activation in inflammatory disease.

## Open problems

- Disentangling the erythropoietic versus anti-inflammatory consequences of HIF activation for safe drug repurposing.

## Relevance to active research

EPO is the prototypical physiological output of HIF signalling and the clinical proof that HIF-activating drugs are tolerable in humans, a translational anchor in [[regulation-immunity-inflammation-hypoxia-immunological-niches]].
