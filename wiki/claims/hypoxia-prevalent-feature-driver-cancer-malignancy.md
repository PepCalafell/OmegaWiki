## Statement

Tumor hypoxia (intratumoral pO₂ < 10 mmHg / 1.3 kPa) is a near-universal feature of solid tumors, arising from a mismatch between accelerated tumor cell growth and a structurally aberrant, leaky, often-collapsing tumor vasculature. Hypoxia is a major driver of cancer malignancy and poor patient prognosis: it contributes to genome instability, autophagy, metabolic reprogramming, angiogenesis, migration, invasion, ECM remodeling, EMT, stem-cell maintenance, immune evasion, and therapy resistance. This is one of the field-consensus statements that motivates the broader literature on tumor hypoxia.

## Conditions and scope

- The hypoxic threshold of pO₂ < 10 mmHg is operational, not absolute; tumor cells can rewire transcription at higher pO₂ (physioxia ~5%) compared to in-vitro 21% normoxia.
- Hypoxia is not uniformly distributed: oxygen gradients exist between perivascular normoxic regions, midzone hypoxic regions, and pannecrotic anoxic regions.
- Even brief exposure of tumor cells to ambient air (minutes) is enough to induce signaling alterations.

## Counter-evidence

- A small subset of cancers (highly vascularized, slow-growing) may be relatively normoxic.
- Better-prognosis colon cancer paradoxically tolerates higher M2/HIF-1α-high tumors — but the underlying mechanism (e.g. SIRPα-CD47 paradox) preserves hypoxia's prevalence while modifying its directional effect.
