## Statement

Kupffer cells (resident liver macrophages) phagocytose senescent or damaged red blood cells (RBCs) and recycle the released iron back to systemic circulation via the ferroportin transporter. This pathway accounts for the great majority of daily iron turnover in mammals (~25 mg/day in humans, vs ~1-2 mg/day absorbed from diet). Under high iron-recycling demand (e.g. haemolysis), bone-marrow-derived monocytes are recruited to the liver and transiently differentiate into ferroportin-expressing BMDMs that supplement Kupffer-cell-mediated recycling.

## Evidence summary

[[papers/physiology-diseases-tissue-resident-macrophages]] reviews the canonical Kupffer iron-recycling axis with mechanistic detail (haem oxygenase 1 degrades haem; ferritin stores intracellular iron; ferroportin exports to plasma transferrin; hepcidin regulates ferroportin levels). The BMDM-takeover mechanism (under high demand) is from Theurl et al. 2016 *Nat Med* (cited in the review).

## Conditions and scope

- Steady-state iron throughput dominated by Kupffer cells.
- High-demand contexts (haemolytic anaemia, blood transfusion, severe infection) recruit BMDMs.
- Hepcidin-driven ferroportin internalization couples inflammatory state (IL-6) to iron sequestration in macrophages — basis of anaemia of chronic inflammation.

## Counter-evidence

- Splenic red pulp macrophages also perform iron recycling (parallel pathway). Splenectomy redistributes load to Kupffer cells.
- Whether Kupffer cells or splenic red pulp macs dominate quantitatively varies across species and physiological states.
