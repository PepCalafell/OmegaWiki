## Statement

Tissue-resident macrophages self-renew locally within their tissues of residence and persist for years (decades in humans) independently of circulating monocyte input under homeostasis. Microglia, Kupffer cells, alveolar macrophages, and Langerhans cells are the most extensively documented examples. Inflammation can transiently recruit BMDMs but does not necessarily replace the resident pool, and surviving residents can repopulate by local proliferation.

## Evidence summary

The evidence reviewed in [[papers/physiology-diseases-tissue-resident-macrophages]] includes parabiosis experiments (separate circulations show TRMs maintained from local pool), lineage-tracing (Csf1r-creER, Cx3cr1-creER, Ms4a3-cre), and human transplant studies showing donor-derived macrophages persisting years post-transplant in skin, heart, lung, and liver. The dominant interpretation is that TRMs are long-lived self-renewing cells.

## Conditions and scope

- Homeostatic steady-state; many inflammatory and stress contexts disrupt the picture.
- Notable exceptions: gut lamina propria macrophages are continuously replaced by BMDMs after weaning (HSC-derived); some skin and intestinal macrophages also replace partially over time.
- Whole-body irradiation followed by bone marrow transplantation can replace TRMs with BMDMs in some tissues (technical artefact rather than physiological outcome).

## Counter-evidence

- Some tissues (gut lamina propria, post-irradiation tissues) show substantial BMDM contribution.
- Whether self-renewal is by mitosis of differentiated macrophages or by an unidentified tissue progenitor compartment is unresolved (Lazarov 2023 explicitly flags this).
- Reports of mixed-origin populations in human organs after long-term transplant suggest the dichotomy is gradient-like, not absolute.

## Open questions

- Whether local self-renewal is achieved by mitosis of differentiated macrophages or via a yet-unidentified macrophage progenitor (Lazarov 2023 raises this explicitly).
- How TRM self-renewal capacity declines with age and whether age-related TRM loss contributes to organ-specific aging phenotypes.
- Whether long-term-resident BMDMs can acquire bona-fide TRM gene-expression programmes (relevant for cancer immunotherapy).
